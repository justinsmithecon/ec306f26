Goals of This Section

Goals of This Section

In this section we will:

  • Show how wage competition sets the wage and employment in a competitive labour market
  • Use shifts in supply and demand to explain changes in wages and employment
  • Ask whether unemployment, and unequal pay for similar workers, mean the model is wrong
  • See how product market power affects labour demand, and when workers share in profits
  • Solve a linear model for the equilibrium, and find out who really pays a payroll tax

This chapter puts the labour supply model from Chapter 3 and the labour demand model from Chapter 8 together in one market. The first half asks how a competitive market sets the wage, and whether real labour markets look like that. The second half adds product market power, then uses a simple linear model to answer a policy question: are payroll taxes “job killers”? Hold that question; the answer comes at the end.

Introduction

Bringing Supply and Demand Together

  • Labour supply from Chapter 3 and labour demand from Chapter 8 together set the wage and employment in one labour market
  • A labour market is one occupation in one area: hairdressers in Greater Montreal, carpenters in Metro Vancouver

Key Definitions

Perfectly competitive labour market: switching employers costs workers nothing, and hiring or dismissing workers costs firms nothing

Real wage: the nominal wage divided by the price of what workers buy, \(w = W/P\)

  • So everyone takes the wage as given. A firm that pays even a little less than the going wage loses all its workers to a rival
  • We start with competitive product markets too, and relax that later in the chapter
  • The wage in this chapter is the real wage, what the wage buys. We write it \(w\); the Chapter 8 slides used \(W\) for the nominal wage

Chapter 12 relaxes competition on the buyers’ side of the labour market (monopsony), and Chapter 16 on the sellers’ side (unions). Relaxing it matters a lot for policies like the minimum wage.

Why the real wage. Workers care about what their pay buys, so labour supply depends on W/P, where P is the price of the things workers consume. Firms care about the wage relative to the price of what they sell: Chapter 8’s hiring rule p × MPP_N = W can be written MPP_N = W/p, where p is the firm’s own output price. Those are two different prices. And the workers in one market buy different things, and the firms in it sell different things. The book gets around this by assuming a single average price P, measured like the CPI, stands in for all of them. Adding up many workers and firms into one supply curve and one demand curve takes strong assumptions; the book calls this the “aggregation problem” and sets it aside, and so will we. It gets trickier still when a firm has product market power, since then its price depends on how much it sells.

So when the wage rises in this chapter, read it as: the nominal wage rose relative to the average price of what people buy.

Competitive Market Equilibrium

Adding Up Firms and Workers

  • Kapuskasing is a town of about 8,000 people in northern Ontario. Many retailers there hire cashiers
  • Market demand: add up the hours every firm wants at each wage
Hourly wage Tim Hortons Walmart Canadian Tire All firms in town
$18 440 720 980 5,130
$22 340 650 870 4,630
  • Firms want different amounts because their \(MRP_N\) differs, for example because they have different capital
  • Each firm is small, so it takes the market wage as given and hires until \(MRP_N\) equals it
  • Market supply: add up the hours every worker offers at each wage
    • Some cut their hours at high wages, when the income effect wins (Chapter 3)
    • But higher wages also pull new workers in, the extensive margin, so market supply keeps rising
  • From $18 to $22, the elasticity of market demand is about −0.5 and of market supply about 0.26

The full tables in the book run from $10 to $30 (Tables 11.1 and 11.2). The numbers are made up for the example. When the wage rises from $18 to $22, market demand falls from 5,130 to 4,630 hours: a 20% wage rise (measured from the midpoint) cuts hours by about 10%, an elasticity of about −0.5. As in Chapter 8, that is substitution (self-checkout machines replacing cashiers) and scale (stores cutting their opening hours as labour gets dearer). Market supply rises from 5,320 to 5,610 hours, an elasticity of about 0.26.

On the supply side, the book follows three workers. Melanie and Alain work fewer hours once the wage passes $26, where the income effect wins for them. Emma doesn’t work at low wages and joins as the wage rises. Adding up over everyone in town, the new entrants outweigh the people cutting back, so market supply slopes up.

For the board, the book’s Figure 11.1: draw two firms, one with steep labour demand and one with flat labour demand, each facing a horizontal line at the market wage w_c. That line is each firm’s labour supply: it can hire as many workers as it wants at w_c, and none for less. Market demand adds the firms’ quantities at each wage, a horizontal sum, and market demand and supply together set w_c.

Two slips in the book’s tables. Table 11.1 prints market demand at $10 as 6,639; the text uses 6,630, which is what makes the shortage at $10 come to 2,110 hours, so the chart on the next slide uses 6,630. Table 11.2 has Emma working 9 hours at $14 and none at $18, which doesn’t fit the text’s story that higher wages draw her in. Neither affects the market supply column.

Wage Competition Clears the Market

Key Definition

Equilibrium wage: the wage at which the quantity of labour demanded equals the quantity supplied

  • At $22: a surplus of 980 hours. Workers short of hours offer to work for less, and the wage falls
  • At $10: a shortage of 2,110 hours. Firms bid workers away from each other, and the wage rises
  • Competition stops only at equilibrium: here between $14 and $18, closer to $18
  • Adam Smith’s invisible hand: no one sets the wage. Competition does

Name who competes with whom. When jobs are scarce, workers compete with each other; when workers are scarce, firms compete with each other.

Why the wage settles: each move shrinks the gap. A lower wage cuts the hours offered and raises the hours wanted.

The book’s stories. At $22, Melanie can’t get the hours she wants, so she offers Walmart her 44 hours a week at $18. She’s just as productive as any other cashier in town, so Walmart cuts its costs and raises its profit by taking the offer. Other underemployed workers follow. The lower wage makes some workers cut their hours (Alain, perhaps) and some stop working (Emma), and makes firms want more hours. At $18 the surplus is down to 190 hours. At $10 it runs the other way: Canadian Tire puts up a sign offering $14, and since jobs are identical and switching is free, cashiers move. Other stores follow to keep their workers. At $14 the shortage is down to 870 hours.

Drawing straight lines between the table’s rows puts the equilibrium at about $17.30 and 5,250 hours, but the table only tells us it’s between $14 and $18.

The book calls Smith the world’s earliest economic thinker, which overstates it; he’s usually called the founder of modern economics.

iClicker Question

Walmart in Kapuskasing installs self-checkouts and now wants 300 fewer cashier hours at every wage. What happens at Tim Hortons?

A) Nothing, because Tim Hortons’ demand for cashiers hasn’t changed
B) The market wage falls, and Tim Hortons hires more cashier hours
C) The market wage falls, and Tim Hortons hires fewer cashier hours
D) Walmart’s wage falls, but the other stores’ wages stay the same
E) The wage stays the same, and 300 hours of unemployment last

Answer: B. Walmart’s cut lowers market demand, so at the old wage there’s a surplus of cashier hours. Cashiers compete for work, and the market wage falls until supply equals demand again. Tim Hortons’ own demand curve hasn’t moved, but it takes the market wage as given, so at the lower wage it hires more hours: a movement along its demand curve. Total cashier hours in town still fall.

C is the tempting one: it reads the lower wage as Tim Hortons wanting fewer cashiers, a shift, when it’s a movement along the curve. A and D forget that a competitive market has one wage: if Walmart paid less than the other stores, its cashiers would leave. E skips wage competition; the surplus doesn’t last.

Efficient, but Not Necessarily Fair

Key Definition

First Theorem of Welfare Economics: a competitive equilibrium is efficient. No other wage makes firms and workers, taken together, better off

  • Efficient is not the same as fair
    • If supply is large or demand weak, the equilibrium wage can fall below what someone needs to live on
    • Employers can still do well in that market
  • Raising the wage with a minimum wage helps equity, but risks some of that efficiency
  • That efficiency–equity trade-off is at the heart of debates over how far governments should rely on markets to divide up income
  • Minimum wages come back in Chapter 12, once firms have some power to set wages

What “efficient” means here. Each hour of work is worth its MRP_N to the firm and costs the wage, so the firm gains the difference. The worker gets the wage but would have worked for anything above their reservation wage, so the worker gains that difference. At the equilibrium wage, every hour that’s worth more to a firm than to the worker who supplies it gets worked, and no other hour does. At any other wage, the short side of the market decides how much gets hired, some of those mutually beneficial hours go unworked, and the total gain is smaller.

The book draws these gains in Figure 11.1: the firms’ gain is the triangle between the demand curve and the wage, and the workers’ gain is the triangle between the wage and the supply curve. We’ll keep to the words; the areas are for a public finance course.

The point to land: efficiency is about the size of the total gain, not who gets it. A market can be efficient and still leave workers poor.

Change in Supply or Demand

Delivery Drivers in Toronto After the Pandemic

  • Supply shifts right, 1 to 2: record numbers of international students arrived in 2022, and from October 2022 they could work more than 20 hours a week off campus. Flexible delivery work suits them
  • Demand shifts right, 2 to 3: shopping and restaurant meals moved online, and retailers bought delivery vehicles
    • Every vehicle needs a driver, so more vehicles raise each driver’s \(MPP_N\), and demand for drivers rises
  • More supply alone lowers the wage; more demand alone raises it
  • Together: employment rises, and here the real wage ends where it started

How the book sets this up, which we’re leaving off the slide. Coming out of the pandemic, prices rose fast: 8.1% over the year to June 2022, the highest inflation in Canada in 40 years. Nominal wages in most markets didn’t keep up, so the real wage W/P fell below equilibrium, and a real wage below equilibrium means firms want more hours than workers offer. The book ties this to the more than 1 million job vacancies Statistics Canada reported in 2022. That step is a story about sticky nominal wages, which sits outside the competitive model: in the model a shortage gets competed away. One slip in the book’s version: it says rising prices raised firms’ MRP_N. That’s true in dollars, but the curves here are drawn against the real wage, and when all prices rise together the real labour demand curve doesn’t move. The shortage comes from the real wage falling, a movement along the curves. (The book also dates the 1 million vacancies to July 2022 here and to June 2022 in the next section.)

In the book’s telling, the supply shift is what kept the shortage from bidding the wage back up, and the demand shift then pushed it back up, restoring the original real wage with more drivers employed.

Delivery drivers are a good example of a market defined by occupation: drivers for Amazon and for Uber Eats are in the same labour market. Self-driving delivery vehicles are still rare, which is why the vans and the drivers go together.

The book stresses that this is simplified. The two shifts overlapped in time, so the real wage may have barely moved while employment grew. And the shifts don’t have to be the same size, which is the next slide.

Does the Wage or Employment Move More?

Shift Wage Employment
Demand rises ↑ ↑
Supply rises ↓ ↑
Both rise Depends on which shift is bigger ↑
  • How a shift splits between the wage and jobs depends on the elasticity of the other curve
    • Demand rises and supply is inelastic: few extra workers come forward, so the wage rises a lot and employment a little
    • Demand rises and supply is elastic: mostly more jobs, with little change in the wage
  • In Toronto: if drivers’ supply was inelastic, or the demand shift was the bigger one, the wage ended above where it started
  • This logic comes back twice today, with product market power and with payroll taxes

Why elasticity decides it. Inelastic supply means a steep supply curve: to get many more hours, firms have to raise the wage a lot, so most of a demand increase shows up in the wage. With elastic supply, a small wage rise brings out lots of extra hours, so most of it shows up in employment. The same works from the other side: a supply increase lowers the wage a lot when labour demand is inelastic.

The book’s version for Toronto: if drivers’ supply is less elastic than demand, redraw the supply curve steeper, and the demand increase pushes the wage above w₁. Employers would have had to offer bigger raises to attract more drivers.

Review Question 3 at the end of the chapter (robot vacuums and residential cleaners) runs exactly this logic in reverse, so leave it to them.

A new college in Montreal starts graduating hundreds of extra hairdressers a year. People barely get more haircuts when prices fall, so the demand for hairdressers is inelastic. In the Greater Montreal market for hairdressers:

A) The wage falls a lot, and employment rises a little
B) The wage falls a little, and employment rises a lot
C) The wage and employment both fall
D) The wage doesn’t change, because the demand for hairdressers hasn’t changed
E) Employment doesn’t change, because the demand for hairdressers hasn’t changed

Answer: A. More graduates shift supply to the right. At the old wage there’s a surplus, and the wage falls. With inelastic demand, salons hire only a few more hours even when the wage falls a lot, so most of the shift shows up in the wage. It’s the rule from the last slide, run from the other side: a supply shift splits between the wage and employment according to the elasticity of demand.

B is the rule backwards: it’s what happens when demand is elastic. D and E confuse a shift of demand with a movement along it. The demand curve hasn’t moved, but salons hire more hours as the wage falls. C treats more supply as less.

Implications of the Competitive Market Equilibrium

Implication 1: No Lasting Unemployment or Shortages

  • The model: competition continues until the market clears, so neither unemployment nor shortages last
  • The data: Canada’s unemployment rate has never come close to zero since the Labour Force Survey began in 1976
    • Its lowest annual rate was 5.3%, in 2022
  • Shortages are real too: job vacancies passed 1 million in 2022, as baby boomers retire
  • Unemployed workers and unfilled jobs at the same time. Is the model wrong?

This slide is the facts. The next two slides give the explanations.

The model’s claim is about the long run: given enough time, wages adjust until the market clears. The data say unemployment never goes away. In recent years, the bigger worry for Canadian policymakers has been the opposite problem, labour shortages, as baby boomers retire.

The book says the monthly unemployment rate has never been below 4.5%, a low it puts in December 2022. I couldn’t confirm that figure, so the chart uses annual averages from the Labour Force Survey file we used in Chapter 2. Monthly rates move around more than annual averages, so the monthly low is below 5.3%; the point doesn’t depend on the exact number.

Implication 2: Equal Pay for Equally Productive Workers

  • The model: workers doing the same work in the same market earn the same wage
  • Example: Hearst is 96 km west of Kapuskasing. Suppose Kapuskasing pays cashiers more, workers know it, and getting there is free
    • Hearst cashiers offer to work in Kapuskasing for a bit less than Kapuskasing cashiers get, so the Kapuskasing wage falls
    • Hearst loses cashiers, so Hearst stores raise wages to keep them
    • This continues until the two wages are equal
  • Evidence that fits: Alberta’s resource boom after 2003 raised wages as far away as Newfoundland. The option of commuting to an Alberta job let workers bargain for more at home (Green et al. 2019)
  • Evidence that doesn’t: Costco has long paid its cashiers more than Walmart, and gender and racial pay gaps have lasted for decades

The book’s thought experiment imagines a free high-speed train between the two towns, so moving costs nothing.

Green, Morissette, Sand and Snoddy (2019) is the evidence that fits. Long-distance commuting to the Alberta oil fields gave labourers in Newfoundland a new outside option, so Newfoundland employers had to raise wages to keep them. One high-wage job in Alberta helped not just the person who took it, but the many workers who could have taken it and used it to bargain for more where they were. Why it matters: competition links labour markets thousands of kilometres apart, and that is exactly the force that’s supposed to wipe out wage gaps.

The evidence that doesn’t fit: Costco and Walmart hire cashiers in the same towns. And wage gaps for women and some racialized groups have lasted despite decades of policy aimed at them. Chapter 15 covers these gaps.

Note the condition in the title: equally productive. The next slide picks that up.

Is the Model Wrong, or Are the Data?

  • Markets in motion: supply and demand shift constantly, so the data may be snapshots of markets on their way to a new equilibrium. But wage gaps last too long for that to be the whole story
  • Unemployed at what wage? Someone counted as unemployed may want more than the market wage. A firm with a vacancy may be offering less. Surveys don’t ask about wages, and no one is unemployed at the market wage
  • Workers and jobs differ: in skills and work ethic, and in stress, flexibility, benefits and safety. Our data miss much of this

Key Definition

Compensating wage differentials: wage differences that make up for differences in the non-wage features of jobs

  • Not perfectly competitive: licences for doctors and engineers, minimum wages and unions, imperfect information, and the costs of changing jobs or hiring
  • The real question: is the model a good enough approximation? Chapter 12 asks whether employers have power to set wages

These are explanations, not facts. The first three say the model may be fine and the data are measuring something else; the fourth says the model’s assumptions don’t hold exactly.

Board sketch for the second point. On the supply curve, the workers to the right of equilibrium employment have reservation wages above w. If one of them looks for work and doesn’t find a job at a wage they’d accept, the Labour Force Survey counts them as unemployed, but they aren’t willing to work at w. Likewise, firms on the demand curve to the right of equilibrium would hire only at a wage below w*, and their unfilled vacancies show up in the vacancy data. Statistics Canada doesn’t ask either group what wage they’d accept or pay. So the data can pick up these parts of the curves, and that isn’t inconsistent with no unemployment and no shortage at the market wage.

On the first point, Chapter 15 shows that many wage differentials are very persistent, too persistent to be explained by markets adjusting.

On the fourth, economists don’t think most labour markets are perfectly competitive. There’s no consensus on whether the model is still a good enough approximation, and a fast-growing literature asks how much power employers have to set wages. Chapter 12 reviews it.

iClicker Question

Two equally skilled welders in Alberta. One works at remote oil sands sites, on two-week rotations away from home, for $55 an hour. The other works in an Edmonton fabrication shop for $38 an hour. What does this gap tell us about the competitive model?

A) It contradicts implication 2: equally productive workers should earn the same wage
B) It fits the model: the higher wage makes up for the worse working conditions
C) It fits the model only if the oil sands welder is more productive
D) The market is still adjusting, and the gap will close over time
E) It shows that the oil sands employer has the power to set wages

Answer: B. This is a compensating wage differential. Implication 2 is about the same work, and these jobs differ in ways workers care about: weeks away from home, remote camps. If the two jobs paid the same, welders would choose Edmonton, oil sands employers would face a shortage, and competition would push their wage up until the gap made up for the rotation. So the gap is what the competitive model predicts.

A is the tempting one: it reads “equally productive” as “doing the same job”. D expects the gap to close, but a compensating gap is part of the equilibrium, so it lasts. C: the stem says they’re equally skilled, and the gap doesn’t need a productivity difference. E: no wage-setting power is needed, since many employers competing for welders produce this gap. Employer power is Chapter 12’s question.

Imperfect Competition in the Product Market

Product Market Power Lowers Labour Demand

  • Labour markets are defined by occupation, not industry. Drivers deliver food or clothes; banks and schools both hire IT technicians
    • So a firm can have power where it sells, but be one of many where it hires
  • From Chapter 8: a monopolist restricts its output to raise its price. Less output needs less labour, and \(MRP_N = MR_Q \times MPP_N\) is below \(p \times MPP_N\) at every \(N\)
  • Market labour demand adds up firms’ demands, so more product market power means lower labour demand
  • If product markets become less competitive over time, labour demand falls:
    • Wages fall more if labour supply is inelastic
    • Jobs fall more if labour supply is elastic
  • But a monopolist can’t mark down the wage. If it pays less than the going wage, its workers leave

Who is on which side. The owners keep the monopoly profit. Workers in that occupation lose through lower demand for their labour, which shows up as a lower market wage or fewer jobs. They don’t lose through a markdown at their own firm: the firm still pays the going wage, because it’s one of many employers hiring that kind of worker.

The same logic holds for any firm with some power over its price, including the monopolistic competition and oligopoly cases from introductory economics: MR_Q is below the price, so labour demand is lower than it would be if the firm took its price as given. Chapter 8’s slide “Market Power Lowers Labour Demand” has the graph.

The split between wages and jobs is the elasticity rule from the last section: lower demand with inelastic supply mostly cuts wages, and with elastic supply mostly cuts jobs.

The book adds that there are reasons a firm with product market power may pay more than the going wage. That’s the next slide. Review Question 5 and Problem 4 at the end of the chapter work through this, so leave the details to them.

iClicker Question

A city gives one company the only licence to run taxis. It hires drivers in a market that also includes ride-hailing, delivery and courier firms. In the competitive model, compared with many competing taxi companies, the licensed company:

A) Pays its drivers less than the going wage, because it has monopoly power
B) Pays the going wage, but hires fewer drivers
C) Pays more than the going wage, because it earns monopoly profit
D) Pays the going wage, and hires the same number of drivers
E) Hires more drivers, because its profits let it expand

Answer: B. The company has power where it sells rides, but it’s one of many employers where it hires drivers. If it paid less than the going wage, its drivers would leave for ride-hailing or delivery jobs. A monopolist restricts output to raise its price, so it carries fewer passengers and needs fewer drivers: lower labour demand at the going wage.

A is the classic mistake: power in the product market isn’t power over the wage. D forgets that less output needs less labour. E gets it backwards, since a monopolist restricts output. C is wrong in the competitive model, where the market sets the wage. But hold onto it: the next slide shows that in practice, workers often do get a share of the profit.

Rent Sharing

  • A monopolist that pays the going wage keeps all its monopoly profit, or rent
  • But workers often get a share. Unions bargain for one (Chapter 16), and even non-union workers earn more where profits are higher

Key Definition

Rent sharing: workers in competitive labour markets capturing part of the profit their employer earns from product market power

  • Hard to measure: higher pay at profitable firms could reflect more productive workers, or jobs that are worse in ways the data miss
  • So researchers use changes in profits that the workers didn’t cause:
    • US bank deregulation: as states let competing banks in, bank wages fell, 12% for men and 3% for women
    • Patents: where patents were granted rather than rejected, workers got about 30% of the extra profit, more for men and the highest paid
    • UK firms: strong rent sharing, but much less in the 2000s than in the 1980s and 1990s
  • Why it matters: pay depends on the employer’s profits, not just productivity. That breaks implication 2

Blanchflower, Oswald and Sanfey (1996) and Hildreth and Oswald (1997) find that non-union workers earn more in industries and firms where profits are higher.

Black and Strahan (2001). Until the mid-1970s, US regulations kept banks out of each other’s markets. Over the next 25 years, states removed those barriers at different times, so researchers can compare states that had deregulated with states that hadn’t yet. Average wages of bank employees fell after deregulation, so some of the rents that regulation created had been going to workers. The bigger fall for men suggests the rents were shared more with men.

Kline, Petkova, Williams and Zidar (2019) compare US firms whose patent applications were approved with similar firms whose applications were rejected. Approval raised profits and wages; workers captured about 30% of the increase in profits that came from the patent. The share was bigger for men and for the highest-paid employees. Their explanation is that these workers are costly for the firm to replace, which isn’t what a perfectly competitive labour market would predict, since there every worker can be replaced at the going wage.

Bell, Bukowski and Machin (2024) find strong rent sharing in British firms, but it fell a lot over time: workers in the 2000s benefited less from their employers’ success than workers in the 1980s and 1990s. They can’t say what changed in how firms set pay.

A slip in the book: it says Bell et al. follow “Black and Strahan’s approach” of comparing firms that did and didn’t receive patents. Black and Strahan used bank deregulation; comparing firms that did and didn’t get patents is Kline et al.’s design.

Why it matters, in one line: in a perfectly competitive labour market, a firm’s profits shouldn’t affect what it pays, because the market sets the wage. Rent sharing is evidence that firms have some room to set pay, which is Chapter 12’s question.

Working with Supply and Demand

Solving for the Equilibrium

  • To judge a policy, we often need numbers: what does it do to the wage, and to employment?

Key Definitions

Endogenous variables: determined inside the model, here \(w\), \(N^S\) and \(N^D\)

Exogenous variables: taken as given from outside the model. \(X\) shifts supply and \(Z\) shifts demand

Reduced form: equations that give each endogenous variable in terms of the exogenous ones

\[N^S = a + bw + \delta X, \quad b > 0 \qquad\qquad N^D = c + fw + \theta Z, \quad f < 0\]

  • Set \(N^S = N^D\) and solve for the reduced form:

\[w^* = \frac{a-c}{f-b} + \frac{\delta}{f-b}X - \frac{\theta}{f-b}Z \qquad\qquad N^* = \frac{af-bc}{f-b} + \frac{f\delta}{f-b}X - \frac{b\theta}{f-b}Z\]

  • Example: if \(X\) is the cost of child care, \(\delta < 0\). Cheaper child care raises \(N^*\) and lowers \(w^*\)

The derivation, for the board: a + bw + δX = c + fw + θZ, so (b − f)w = c − a + θZ − δX. Dividing through, and writing the denominator as f − b, gives the wage equation. Put w* back into the supply equation to get N*. Note that f − b is negative, since f < 0 and b > 0.

The two equations on the slide are linear supply and demand. In the child care example, cheaper child care lowers parents’ reservation wages and raises labour supply, so N* rises and w* falls.

The child care example with signs. Raising X by one unit changes w* by δ/(f − b), which is positive, since both δ and f − b are negative. It changes N* by fδ/(f − b), which is negative: fδ is positive and f − b is negative. So cutting child care costs lowers the wage and raises employment. The wage falls because more labour supply creates a surplus at the old wage, and workers compete the wage down.

Three slips in the book’s version of this section. First, its general form (equation 11.1) puts Z in supply and X in demand, and its linear version (11.2) swaps them; we use 11.2 throughout. Second, it writes the effects of cheaper child care as fδ/(f − b) > 0 on employment and δ/(f − b) < 0 on the wage. With δ < 0, f < 0 and f − b < 0, those two expressions have the opposite signs; the effects of a one-unit cut are −fδ/(f − b) > 0 and −δ/(f − b) < 0. Its conclusions are right. Third, it says the demand curve’s slope is 1/b and its intercept −a/b. With w on the vertical axis, those are the supply curve’s; demand’s slope is 1/f and its intercept −c/f. It looks like a leftover from the previous edition, which wrote demand as a + bw.

Linear curves have a constant slope but not a constant elasticity: the supply elasticity is b × w/N and the demand elasticity f × w/N, which change as you move along the curve. Researchers often have elasticity estimates from other studies, so they use log-linear curves instead: log N^S = λ + η log w + δX and log N^D = α + β log w + θZ. Then η is the supply elasticity and β the demand elasticity, the same at every point, and the model solves exactly as in the linear case. The book says the elasticities are “β and η, respectively”, which reverses them.

A Worked Example

  • Supply: \(N^S = 60 + w\). Demand: \(N^D = 120 - 2w\)
  • Set them equal: \(60 + w = 120 - 2w\), so \(3w = 60\)
    • \(w^* = 20\) and \(N^* = 80\)
  • Same answer from the formula, with \(a = 60\), \(b = 1\), \(c = 120\), \(f = -2\): \(w^* = \dfrac{60 - 120}{-2 - 1} = 20\)
  • Cheaper child care: supply rises by 6 hours at every wage, so \(N^S = 66 + w\)
    • \(66 + w = 120 - 2w\), so \(w^* = 18\) and \(N^* = 84\): more employment at a lower wage
  • Elasticities at the equilibrium: supply \(1 \times \dfrac{20}{80} = 0.25\), demand \(-2 \times \dfrac{20}{80} = -0.5\). Close to the cashier market

Employment from the formula: N* = (af − bc)/(f − b) = (60 × (−2) − 1 × 120)/(−3) = −240/−3 = 80.

To draw it with w on the vertical axis: supply is w = N − 60 and demand is w = 60 − N/2.

Keep these numbers handy: the payroll tax slides use them.

Problem 3 at the end of the chapter works a similar example with different numbers, so leave that one to them.

Application: Incidence of a Payroll Tax

Are Payroll Taxes Job Killers?

Key Definition

Pure payroll tax: a tax on employers in proportion to their total wage bill. British Columbia, Manitoba, Ontario, Quebec, and Newfoundland and Labrador have one

  • More common are CPP/QPP and EI premiums, workers’ compensation, and some provincial health levies. Your pay stub splits these between you and your employer, at least on paper
  • Critics call payroll taxes job killers: they raise the cost of hiring
  • The model: employers pay a tax of \(T\) per hour. The firm pays \(w + T\): \(w\) to the worker and \(T\) to the government
  • Step by step:
    • The most a firm will pay a worker for the \(N\)th hour is now \(MRP_N - T\)
    • So the demand curve, measured in the wage workers get, shifts down by exactly \(T\) at every \(N\)
    • At the old wage, firms want fewer hours: a surplus of workers. Wage competition pushes the wage down until supply equals demand again

Ontario’s version is the Employer Health Tax. British Columbia’s, under the same name, started in 2019 and is missing from the book’s list.

Why the shift is exactly T. Before the tax, the Nth hour was worth MRP_N to the firm, so that was the most it would pay for it. With the tax, that hour costs w + T, so the most the firm will now pay the worker is MRP_N − T. That’s true at every N, so the whole demand curve, drawn against the wage workers receive, drops by T. In the linear model from the last section, demand becomes N^D = c + f(w + T) + θZ.

The critics’ argument assumes the firm’s cost goes up by the full T. The question for the next slide is whether it does, once the wage adjusts.

Who Really Pays?

  • Before the tax: point A. Wage 20, 80 hours
  • After a $6 tax: point B. Workers get 16, firms pay \(16 + 6 = 22\), and hours fall to 76
  • Workers bear \(\dfrac{20 - 16}{6} = \dfrac{2}{3}\) of the tax, through a lower wage
  • Firms bear \(\dfrac{22 - 20}{6} = \dfrac{1}{3}\), through a higher cost of labour

Key Definitions

Tax incidence: who bears a tax

  • Statutory: who is legally required to pay it. Here, employers
  • Economic: who actually ends up worse off. Here, shared, mostly workers

Walk through the graph. At A, before the tax, the wage is 20 and 80 hours are worked. The tax drops demand by 6 at every N. If the wage stayed at 20, firms would want only N′ = 68 hours, but workers still offer 80: a surplus of 12. Workers compete, the wage falls, and the market settles at B: workers get 16, the firm pays 16 + 6 = 22, and 76 hours are worked.

In the book’s letters: BD is the full tax, T. BC, from 16 up to 20, is the part workers bear through the lower wage. CD, from 20 up to 22, is the part firms bear through the higher cost of each hour. Workers’ share is BC/BD.

The key lesson: the tax falls on workers even though the law puts it entirely on employers. Who fills in the forms doesn’t decide who pays.

Solving for the Equilibrium with the Tax

  • With the tax, an hour of work costs the firm \(w + T\), so labour demand depends on \(w + T\) instead of \(w\)

With the numbers:

  • Supply: \(N^S = 60 + w\)
  • Demand with a $6 tax: \(N^D = 120 - 2(w + 6)\)
  • Set them equal: \(60 + w = 108 - 2w\), so \(3w = 48\)
    • Workers get \(w_1 = 16\)
    • Firms pay \(w_1 + T = 22\)
    • Hours: \(N_1 = 60 + 16 = 76\)

In general:

  • Supply: \(N^S = a + bw\). Demand: \(N^D = c + f(w + T)\)
  • Set them equal: \((b - f)\,w = c - a + fT\)

\[w_1 = \frac{c - a}{b - f} + \frac{f}{b - f}\,T = w_0 + \frac{f}{b - f}\,T\]

  • \(w_0\) is the wage with no tax. Since \(f < 0\), the tax lowers the wage workers get
  • Workers bear \(\dfrac{-f}{b - f}\) of the tax, and firms bear \(\dfrac{b}{b - f}\)

This is the algebra behind the graph on the last slide: A is the no-tax equilibrium, B is where the numbers on the left end up.

Why demand depends on w + T. The firm hires until the cost of an hour equals what the hour brings in. With the tax, the cost of an hour is w + T, so the firm hires the same hours at wage w with the tax as it would have hired at wage w + T without it. That’s the shift down by exactly T from the earlier slide, in algebra.

Check the shares with the numbers: b = 1 and f = −2, so workers bear 2/(1 + 2) = 2/3 of the $6, or $4 (20 down to 16), and firms bear 1/3, or $2 (20 up to 22). The two shares always add to one.

Employment, if anyone asks: N₁ = a + b·w₁ = N₀ + (bf/(b − f))·T, which is negative since f < 0. Here (1 × −2)/3 × 6 = −4, so 80 hours fall to 76.

We leave out the supply and demand shifters, X and Z, because the tax doesn’t change them; they’re part of w₀.

Elasticities Decide the Split

  • From the last slide, workers’ share of the tax is \(\dfrac{-f}{b - f}\). It depends only on the slopes \(b\) and \(f\)
  • With \(T = 6\), and every case starting from \(w = 20\) and 80 hours:
Case \(b\) \(f\) Workers get Firms pay (\(w + T\)) Hours Workers’ share
Inelastic supply 1 −2 16 22 76 2/3
Elastic supply 4 −2 18 24 72 1/3
Inelastic demand (short run) 1 −0.5 18 24 78 1/3
  • Inelastic supply: workers keep working at a lower wage, so they bear more of the tax, and fewer jobs are lost
  • Elastic supply: workers leave when the wage falls, so firms bear more, through lower profits or higher prices, and more jobs are lost
  • Short run vs. long run: labour demand is less elastic in the short run (Chapter 8), so firms bear more at first and fewer jobs are lost. Over time, more of the tax shifts to workers

Check each row with the formula. −f/(b − f) = 2/(1 + 2) = 2/3; 2/(4 + 2) = 1/3; 0.5/(1 + 0.5) = 1/3. The elastic-supply row uses supply N = 4w, and the short-run row uses demand N = 90 − 0.5w; both pass through w = 20 and N = 80, so the three cases start from the same place.

The book’s own example: b = 3 and f = −1, so workers bear a quarter of the tax.

A correction to the book. It says that the more willing workers are to work at lower wages, the steeper the labour supply curve and the bigger b. A steeper supply curve means a smaller b: b is how much N changes when w changes, so on a graph with w on the vertical axis the slope is 1/b. A bigger b means workers bear less. The book’s conclusion, that workers who keep working at lower wages bear more of the tax, is right.

On the short run. Chapter 8 showed that firms can’t swap capital for labour quickly, so labour demand is less elastic in the short run and f is closer to zero. If labour supply is also less elastic in the short run, that prediction may not hold. In the end, who bears a payroll tax is an empirical question, which is the next slide.

When firms bear part of the tax, they can absorb it in lower profits or pass it on to consumers through higher prices.

iClicker Question

In a labour market, supply is \(N^S = a + 2w\) and demand is \(N^D = c - 4w\). The government puts a payroll tax of $3 an hour on employers. In the new equilibrium, the wage workers receive:

A) Doesn’t change, because employers pay the tax
B) Falls by $1
C) Falls by $2
D) Falls by $3
E) Rises by $1

Answer: C. With the tax, demand becomes N = c − 4(w + 3). Setting supply equal to demand: a + 2w = c − 4w − 12, so 6w = c − a − 12 and w = (c − a)/6 − 2. Before the tax, w = (c − a)/6, so the wage falls by $2. Or use the formula from the last slide: workers’ share is −f/(b − f) = 4/(2 + 4) = 2/3, and 2/3 of $3 is $2. Firms now pay w + 3, which is $1 more than before.

A confuses statutory incidence, who sends the money to the government, with economic incidence. B swaps the two shares: $1 is what firms bear. D is right only if supply is perfectly inelastic (b = 0), which is close to what Deslauriers et al. find on the next slide. E is the change in what firms pay, not in what workers get.

Evidence: Workers Bear Most of the Tax

  • Why it’s hard to measure: rate changes are usually small, like 4% to 5% of payroll. And governments may raise rates when labour markets are strong, which makes the taxes look good for jobs
  • Chile, 1981 (Gruber 1997): privatizing public pensions cut the average employer rate from 30% to 8.5%. Almost all of the cut went into higher wages, with little change in employment
  • Canada (Deslauriers et al. 2021): linked tax records for workers and their employers, using rates that differ with payroll size and Manitoba’s 2007 cut for medium-sized firms
    • No effect on employment or output: the tax was passed entirely to workers as lower wages
    • In the model, \(b\) is close to zero, so workers’ share is close to one. That fits the small supply elasticities in Chapter 3
  • Earlier Canadian evidence agrees: job losses are small in the long run (Kesselman 1996)
  • So payroll taxes aren’t job killers, but workers pay them, through lower wages. In return they get the pensions and EI the taxes fund

Why reverse causality matters: if governments raise payroll taxes when the labour market is strong, a naive comparison would show higher taxes alongside higher wages and employment. It would be wrong to conclude that the taxes raised them.

Gruber (1997), the book’s Exhibit 11.1. Before 1981, Chile’s pay-as-you-go pension system, like the CPP/QPP, was funded by payroll taxes averaging 30% on employers and 12% on employees. In May 1981 Chile switched to individual savings accounts, and the average employer rate fell to 8.5%, with workers’ own contributions making up the rest. At the same time, the government ordered firms to raise nominal wages by 18%, but inflation was running at 25%, so the raw numbers don’t show whether real wages rose because of the tax cut. Gruber used firm-level data and compared firms that the change should have affected differently. Why it’s convincing: the change was huge, and it came from pension reform, not from the state of the labour market.

Deslauriers, Dostie, Gagné and Paré link workers’ income tax records to their employers’ corporate tax records. The book’s text dates the study 2024, but its reference list and the journal give 2021 (Canadian Journal of Economics). Why it matters: it’s recent Canadian data, and the result is the extreme case of the model, where workers bear the whole tax.

Kesselman (1996) reviews the Canadian evidence on the payroll taxes that fund public pensions: some job losses in the short run perhaps, small ones in the long run, and the tax falls mostly on workers.

Who is on which side: the law puts the tax on employers; the economics puts most of it on workers. Problem 5 at the end of the chapter takes this a step further: if workers value the benefits a payroll tax pays for, their supply curve shifts too.

Summary

Summary

Competitive market equilibrium:

  • Wage competition moves the wage to where supply equals demand. The result is efficient, but not necessarily fair

Changes in supply or demand:

  • Shifts change the wage and employment. Elasticities decide which moves more

Implications of the competitive model:

  • No lasting unemployment, and equal pay for equal workers. The data don’t fit neatly, because of how we measure, differences between workers and jobs, and imperfect competition

Imperfect competition in the product market:

  • Product market power lowers labour demand. A monopolist still pays the going wage, but workers often share its profits

Working with supply and demand:

  • Linear supply and demand can be solved for the equilibrium wage and employment

Payroll taxes:

  • Who bears a payroll tax depends on elasticities. The evidence says workers bear most of it, and job losses are small
