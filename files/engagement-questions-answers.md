## Introduction (intro)

**1. Think About It**
> If labour economics studies the interaction between workers, employers, and government, what would happen to wages and employment if one of these three players was removed entirely?

**Model answer:** Without government, there would be no minimum wages, employment standards, or social insurance — wages would be set entirely by market forces, which could lead to exploitative outcomes for workers with little bargaining power. Without employers (e.g., everyone is self-employed), there is no wage-setting problem — but coordination and specialization losses would reduce productivity. Without workers, there is no labour market at all. The point is that the interaction of all three actors shapes labour market outcomes.

---

**2. Discussion**
> Why might we observe that men earn more than women on average, even in the same occupation? What are some possible explanations — both economic and non-economic?

**Model answer:** Economic explanations include differences in human capital (experience, tenure, education field), compensating differentials (hours flexibility, dangerous work), and labour market discrimination (taste-based or statistical). Non-economic explanations include social norms around negotiation, household division of labour affecting career continuity, and institutional factors (parental leave design, networking). The key insight is that the raw gap conflates many factors — decomposing it is a central task of labour economics.

---

**3. Think About It**
> What would happen to wages and employment in long-term care facilities if the government made it difficult for nurses to switch between the hospital and long-term care sectors?

**Model answer:** Restricting mobility segments the market. Long-term care facilities would effectively face a less elastic labour supply — workers cannot easily leave for hospitals. This gives facilities more monopsony-like power: they could pay lower wages without losing as many workers. Employment might fall (fewer workers willing to enter/stay in the sector at lower wages) and quality of care could decline. This illustrates why competitive equilibrium assumes free mobility between sectors.

---

**4. Discussion**
> Pick one of the policy issues listed above. How would you design a study to measure its effect on the labour market? What data would you need?

**Model answer:** Answers will vary by policy chosen. Strong answers identify: (1) a clear outcome variable (wages, employment, hours, participation), (2) a comparison group or identification strategy (before/after, treatment/control, natural experiment), and (3) specific data needs (survey data like the LFS, administrative records, linked employer-employee data). For example, studying immigration's effect on wages requires wage data by region/occupation, immigrant inflow data, and a strategy to account for the fact that immigrants choose where to settle (instrument with historical settlement patterns).

## Labour Supply 1 (lsupply1)

**1. Think About It**
> A full-time student who is 22 years old and not looking for work is excluded from the labour force. What would have to change about their situation for them to be counted as unemployed rather than out of the labour force?

**Model answer:** They would need to actively search for work (e.g., submit job applications, attend interviews) while remaining without a job. Simply wanting a job is not enough — the LFS definition requires active job search in the past four weeks. If they start searching but don't find work, they move from "not in the labour force" to "unemployed." This highlights that the unemployment rate misses discouraged workers who have stopped searching.

---

**2. Discussion**
> Suppose a person receives a large inheritance, increasing their non-labour income substantially. Using the budget constraint and indifference curve framework, what would you predict happens to their reservation wage, and why?

**Model answer:** The inheritance shifts the budget constraint upward (higher consumption at zero hours of work). With higher non-labour income, the person can achieve a higher indifference curve without working. Their reservation wage — the minimum wage at which they would be willing to work — increases because they now require a higher wage to compensate for giving up leisure. If the market wage is below the new reservation wage, they may drop out of the labour force entirely. This is a pure income effect.

---

**3. Think About It**
> A worker currently earning $20/hour gets a raise to $25/hour and decides to work fewer hours per week. Which effect — income or substitution — must be dominant for this worker? What does this tell you about how they value leisure relative to consumption at the margin?

**Model answer:** The income effect must dominate. The substitution effect of a higher wage makes leisure more expensive, encouraging more work. But the income effect of being richer makes the worker want more of all normal goods, including leisure. Since hours fell, the income effect outweighs the substitution effect. At the margin, this worker values additional leisure more than additional consumption — they are on the backward-bending portion of their individual labour supply curve.

---

**4. Discussion**
> Why might we observe that high-income professionals (doctors, lawyers, executives) often work very long hours, even though the backward-bending supply curve predicts that higher wages should eventually lead to fewer hours worked?

**Model answer:** Several explanations: (1) Self-selection — people who have strong preferences for work (low taste for leisure) sort into demanding professions. (2) Career incentives — hours worked today signal commitment and increase future earnings (tournament models). (3) Fixed costs — professional credentials require large upfront human capital investments, making the opportunity cost of not working very high. (4) Peer effects and professional culture. (5) The backward-bending curve is about the response to wage *changes* — these professionals may have always faced high wages and chosen high hours. The empirical evidence suggests the substitution effect dominates at most wage levels.

---

**5. Think About It**
> During the COVID-19 pandemic, many households experienced both job losses (added worker effect) and falling wages in available jobs (discouraged worker effect) at the same time. What would the model predict about labour force participation for secondary earners in these households, and why might the two effects work against each other?

**Model answer:** The added worker effect predicts that when the primary earner loses their job (income falls), secondary earners enter the labour force to compensate — this is an income effect. The discouraged worker effect predicts that when wages fall or job prospects are poor, some workers stop searching because the expected return to search is too low — this is a substitution effect. During COVID, both operated simultaneously: some secondary earners entered the labour force while others withdrew. The net effect depends on which dominates. Empirically, during COVID the discouraged worker effect was stronger initially (participation fell sharply), partly because health risks made job search itself costly.

## Labour Supply 2 (lsupply2)

**1. Think About It**
> If 83% of Canadian families receive some form of government transfer, why is program design so important? What happens if a program is too generous or too stingy?

**Model answer:** Program design matters because transfers alter the budget constraint and therefore labour supply incentives. A program that is too generous (high guarantee, high clawback rate) creates strong work disincentives — the effective wage from working can approach zero, discouraging participation. A program that is too stingy fails to achieve its redistributive goals and may leave vulnerable populations without adequate support. The challenge is balancing income support (reducing poverty) with preserving work incentives (avoiding high effective marginal tax rates). Every design parameter — the guarantee level, the clawback rate, the earnings exemption — has predictable effects on the budget constraint.

---

**2. Discussion**
> Why might someone who was working 20 hours per week before welfare was introduced choose to stop working entirely, while someone working 40 hours per week continues to work the same amount?

**Model answer:** This depends on the shape of the welfare budget constraint and individual preferences. Welfare typically creates a notch: if you earn below the exemption, you keep the full benefit; above it, benefits are clawed back. A 20-hour worker earning modestly might find that the welfare guarantee (at zero hours) puts them on a higher indifference curve than their current work-plus-earnings bundle — especially if the clawback rate is high. A 40-hour worker earning more may already be above the break-even point where welfare is fully clawed back, so the program doesn't change their constraint at all. The key is that welfare's work disincentive is strongest for those whose earnings are close to or below the break-even level.

---

**3. Think About It**
> A negative income tax reduces the benefit as you earn more, while a wage subsidy increases your pay for each hour worked. Which program would you expect to have a larger positive effect on labour force participation among non-workers, and why?

**Model answer:** The wage subsidy. Under a NIT, non-workers receive a guaranteed income without working — this raises their reservation wage and may discourage entry. Under a wage subsidy, you only receive benefits if you work — there is no benefit at zero hours. This means the wage subsidy creates a pure substitution effect toward work (leisure becomes more expensive) without the income effect at zero hours that the NIT creates. Empirically, programs like the EITC (a wage subsidy) have been shown to significantly increase labour force participation, especially among single mothers, while NIT experiments showed modest reductions in work effort.

---

**4. Discussion**
> The EI budget constraint has a segment where the effective wage is only 40% of the market wage. Why might a seasonal worker in the Maritimes choose to work exactly 14 weeks per year under this system?

**Model answer:** EI requires a minimum number of insurable hours (roughly 14 weeks of full-time work in high-unemployment regions) to qualify for benefits. Once qualified, the worker receives 55% of average earnings for up to 36 weeks. Working beyond 14 weeks during the eligible period earns the market wage but means forgoing EI benefits — creating an effective wage of only 40 cents on the dollar (market wage minus forgone EI). Rational workers may cluster at exactly the qualifying threshold: earn enough to qualify, then collect EI for the rest of the year. This is not laziness but a rational response to program incentives — the program effectively creates a very high marginal tax rate on work beyond the qualifying period.

---

**5. Think About It**
> Quebec's $5/day child care program increased mothers' labour force participation by 7.7 percentage points but was associated with negative child outcomes. What does this trade-off suggest about how we should evaluate the success of a child care subsidy policy?

**Model answer:** It suggests that labour supply effects alone are insufficient for evaluating child care policy. A complete evaluation must consider: (1) the labour supply response (positive — more mothers working), (2) child development outcomes (mixed — depends on quality of care and counterfactual), (3) fiscal effects (higher tax revenue from working mothers vs. program costs), and (4) distributional effects (who benefits — universal programs subsidize high-income families too). The negative child outcomes may reflect rapid expansion outpacing quality control, or that the marginal children entering care would have had better outcomes with parental care. This highlights that "getting mothers into the labour force" is not the only policy objective.

## Labour Supply 3 (lsupply3)

**1. Think About It**
> Suppose you observe that 50-year-olds have higher labour force participation than 25-year-olds in 2025. Can you conclude that people work more as they age? Why or why not?

**Model answer:** No. This is a cross-sectional comparison that confounds age effects with cohort effects. The 50-year-olds (born ~1975) and 25-year-olds (born ~2000) grew up in different economic environments with different social norms, education levels, and labour market conditions. The 25-year-olds may be in school longer (lower participation now but higher later). To isolate the true age effect, you need panel data following the same individuals over time, or you need to separately identify age, cohort, and period effects — which requires strong assumptions because age = year - birth year (perfect collinearity).

---

**2. Discussion**
> A government announces today that the minimum wage will increase by 20% starting in two years. Using the life cycle model, how would you expect workers near the minimum wage to adjust their labour supply now versus after the increase takes effect?

**Model answer:** In the life cycle model, workers allocate labour to periods when the wage is highest (intertemporal substitution). If the wage will be higher in two years, the model predicts: (1) Now: workers may slightly reduce hours (substitute toward future work when the wage is higher) — but this effect is small for low-wage workers with liquidity constraints. (2) After the increase: hours worked per period may increase (substitution effect) or decrease (income effect). The announcement also affects human capital decisions — some may invest in training now to be more productive at the higher wage. Key caveat: life cycle models assume perfect capital markets; minimum wage workers are often credit-constrained, limiting their ability to smooth consumption and adjust labor supply intertemporally.

---

**3. Think About It**
> What would happen to a household's use of home-cooked meals versus takeout food if one partner receives a large raise at work? Explain using the household production model.

**Model answer:** In the household production model, home-cooked meals are produced using time (a household input) and market goods (ingredients). The partner's raise increases the opportunity cost of their time spent in household production. The household will substitute away from time-intensive home production toward market substitutes: more takeout, meal kits, or restaurant meals. The partner who received the raise will reallocate time from cooking to market work (where their return is now higher). The other partner may increase their share of cooking if their wage didn't change, illustrating comparative advantage within the household. This is Becker's key insight: household production decisions respond to relative wages just like firm production decisions respond to relative input prices.

---

**4. Discussion**
> Why might we observe that countries with the most generous parental leave policies do not necessarily have the highest fertility rates?

**Model answer:** Fertility decisions depend on many factors beyond parental leave: (1) The opportunity cost of children includes career interruption, which generous leave only partially offsets. (2) Housing costs, child care costs, and education costs may matter more than leave policy. (3) Cultural and social norms about family size evolve independently of policy. (4) The quality-quantity trade-off: as incomes rise, parents invest more per child (quality) rather than having more children (quantity). (5) Contraception access and women's education are strong predictors of fertility that dominate policy effects. (6) Selection: countries may adopt generous leave *because* they have low fertility (reverse causality). The economics of fertility teaches us that children are a "normal good" in some dimensions but the income elasticity is complex — the substitution effect of higher wages (children are more expensive) often dominates the income effect.

---

**5. Think About It**
> If the government reduced CPP benefits but simultaneously eliminated the clawback on OAS for high earners, what would be the combined effect on the retirement age of high-income workers?

**Model answer:** Two opposing effects: (1) Reduced CPP benefits lower retirement wealth, creating an income effect that encourages delayed retirement (work longer to compensate for lower pension income). (2) Eliminating the OAS clawback removes the implicit tax on high earners' post-65 income. Currently, the clawback acts like a high marginal tax rate on income above ~$87K, discouraging work. Removing it increases the effective return to working past 65 for high earners (substitution effect toward more work). Both effects push toward later retirement for high-income workers — this is one of the rare cases where income and substitution effects reinforce each other. The magnitude depends on which program is more important to the worker's retirement planning.

## Labour Demand 1 (ldemand1)

**1. Think About It**
> A firm currently employs workers where pMP_N = w. Suppose the government introduces a per-unit subsidy on the firm's output, raising the effective price the firm receives. What happens to the firm's short-run demand for labour, and why?

**Model answer:** The subsidy raises the effective output price p. Since the firm hires where pMP_N = w, a higher p means that at the current employment level, pMP_N > w — the value of an additional worker exceeds their cost. The firm will hire more workers, moving down the MP_N curve until pMP_N = w is restored at a higher employment level. Graphically, the labour demand curve (which is the pMP_N curve) shifts right/up. This is why output subsidies increase labour demand — they raise the value of each worker's marginal product.

---

**2. Think About It**
> A firm currently satisfies MP_N/w = MP_K/r. Suppose the rental rate of capital r suddenly doubles while the wage w stays the same. In what direction will the firm adjust its mix of labour and capital, and what happens to the isocost line?

**Model answer:** The isocost line becomes steeper (slope = w/r falls in absolute value since r doubled). The firm is no longer at a cost minimum: MP_N/w < MP_K/r at the old input mix, meaning capital is now relatively more expensive per unit of marginal product. The firm substitutes toward labour and away from capital until the equi-marginal condition is restored. The new tangency point has more labour and less capital. This is the substitution effect only — whether total labour demand rises or falls also depends on the scale effect (higher costs may reduce output).

---

**3. Discussion**
> When wages rise, the substitution and scale effects both reduce labour demand, but they have opposing effects on capital demand. Can you think of a real-world industry where the substitution effect on capital would clearly dominate the scale effect, and one where the scale effect might dominate? What makes those industries different?

**Model answer:** Substitution effect dominates: Agriculture or manufacturing where machines can directly replace workers (e.g., automated harvesting, robotic assembly). When wages rise, firms invest heavily in capital because labour and capital are good substitutes — capital demand rises despite higher costs. Scale effect dominates: Personal services like haircuts or elder care where capital is a poor substitute for labour. When wages rise, firms can't easily replace workers with machines, so the main response is to reduce output — capital demand falls along with labour demand. The key difference is the elasticity of substitution between labour and capital.

---

**4. Discussion**
> Why might we observe that labour demand has become more elastic over time in both Canada and the USA? Which of the four factors affecting elasticity do you think has changed the most, and why?

**Model answer:** The four Hicks-Marshall factors are: (1) elasticity of substitution between labour and capital, (2) elasticity of product demand, (3) labour's share of total costs, and (4) elasticity of supply of other inputs. Globalization and technological change have likely increased (1) — automation and offshoring make it easier to substitute away from domestic labour. Trade has increased (2) — firms face more competitive product markets with more elastic demand. The factor that has changed most is arguably the elasticity of substitution: digital technology and global supply chains give firms far more options for replacing or relocating labour than existed 30-40 years ago. This has important implications for policy — a more elastic labour demand means minimum wages and payroll taxes have larger employment effects.

---

**5. Think About It**
> Suppose a Canadian auto parts manufacturer faces a drop in wages in Mexico. Under what conditions would this actually *increase* demand for Canadian auto workers rather than decrease it?

**Model answer:** If Canadian and Mexican auto workers are complements rather than substitutes in production. For example, if Mexican workers do assembly while Canadian workers do design, engineering, or quality control, then cheaper Mexican labour reduces production costs, increases output (scale effect), and raises demand for the complementary Canadian workers. This can happen in vertically integrated supply chains where different stages of production use different types of labour. The key condition is that the scale effect (more total output) outweighs any substitution effect (replacing Canadian workers with Mexican workers). This is more likely when the two types of labour perform different tasks in the production process.

## Labour Demand 2 (ldemand2)

**1. Discussion**
> If non-wage benefits are so advantageous for both firms and workers, why do some firms still offer minimal benefits and rely almost entirely on wage compensation?

**Model answer:** Several reasons: (1) Small firms face higher per-worker administrative costs for benefits programs and lack bargaining power with insurers. (2) Workers in some sectors (e.g., gig economy, retail) have high turnover, making long-term benefits costly and less valued. (3) Worker heterogeneity — young, healthy, single workers may prefer higher wages over health insurance or pensions (different indifference curve slopes). (4) Tax advantages of benefits accrue more to high-income workers (higher marginal tax rates), so low-wage firms see less benefit. (5) Competitive dynamics — if competitors offer all-wage compensation, matching that structure may be necessary to attract workers who comparison-shop on take-home pay. The compensating differential framework predicts that firms offering fewer benefits must pay higher wages to compete.

---

**2. Think About It**
> What would happen to firm hiring behaviour if the government introduced a large new per-worker tax that does not vary with hours worked?

**Model answer:** A per-worker tax is a quasi-fixed cost — it's paid per employee regardless of hours. This increases the fixed cost of each worker relative to the variable cost of hours. Firms respond by: (1) hiring fewer workers but working each one more hours (substitute hours for bodies), (2) increasing use of overtime rather than new hires, (3) shifting toward contractors or temporary workers who may not trigger the tax, and (4) investing more in capital to substitute for labour. The key insight is that per-worker taxes discourage employment more than per-hour taxes of equal revenue, because they distort the hours-workers margin. This is why payroll taxes structured as per-worker levies (like some employer health insurance mandates) can have larger disemployment effects than wage-proportional taxes.

---

**3. Think About It**
> Why might we observe that highly skilled workers are more likely to work overtime, while lower-skilled positions are more likely to be filled by temp or gig workers?

**Model answer:** Quasi-fixed costs explain this pattern. Highly skilled workers have high hiring and training costs (large quasi-fixed costs per worker). When demand increases, it's cheaper for the firm to pay overtime premiums to existing skilled workers than to hire and train additional ones — the quasi-fixed cost of a new skilled hire exceeds the overtime premium. For lower-skilled workers, quasi-fixed costs are low (minimal training, easy replacement), so firms prefer to hire additional temporary workers rather than pay overtime premiums to existing ones. This also explains why skilled workers have more job security during downturns — the firm's sunk quasi-fixed investment makes it costly to lay them off and rehire later.

---

**4. Discussion**
> Given what we know about the lump of labour fallacy, why do politicians and the public still frequently argue that reducing work hours or restricting immigration will create more jobs?

**Model answer:** The lump of labour fallacy assumes a fixed amount of work to be divided among workers. It persists because: (1) It's intuitively appealing — if one person works less, it seems like another person could fill those hours. (2) In the short run and in specific local labour markets, it can appear to hold (partial equilibrium thinking). (3) It ignores general equilibrium effects: reduced hours raise costs, which raise prices, which reduce demand, which can reduce total employment. (4) Immigration restrictions ignore that immigrants are also consumers and entrepreneurs who create demand and jobs. (5) Political incentives — simple narratives win votes even if economically incorrect. The economic evidence consistently shows that work-sharing schemes do not increase total employment and that immigration does not reduce native employment in the long run.

---

## Wages and Employment in a Single Labour Market (lmarket)

**1. Think About It**
> If a new technology increases labour demand in a competitive market, what happens to wages and employment in the short run versus the long run? Why do the outcomes differ?

**Model answer:** Short run: labour supply is relatively inelastic (workers can't easily enter the market). The demand shift primarily raises wages with a modest employment increase. Long run: higher wages attract new workers into the market (more elastic supply). Employment increases further while the wage increase moderates. The outcomes differ because supply elasticity increases over time — in the short run, the number of qualified workers is roughly fixed, but over time people retrain, relocate, or enter the field. The long-run equilibrium has higher wages and employment than the original, but lower wages than the short-run peak.

---

**2. Discussion**
> Why might a monopolist pay lower wages than a competitive firm, even if both firms hire workers from the same labour market?

**Model answer:** A monopolist in the product market restricts output to raise prices, producing where MR = MC rather than P = MC. Because it produces less output, it demands less labour at any given wage. This shifts the labour demand curve left — the monopolist's demand for labour is based on marginal revenue product (MR x MP_N) rather than value of marginal product (P x MP_N). Since MR < P for a monopolist, the labour demand curve lies below the competitive firm's demand curve. In equilibrium, the monopolist hires fewer workers, which (in a market context) means lower wages and employment. The key distinction is that the monopoly power is in the *product* market — the firm may still be a wage-taker in the labour market.

---

**3. Think About It**
> A payroll tax is legally imposed on employers, yet economists say workers bear most of the burden in the long run. What mechanism causes the tax burden to shift from employers to workers over time?

**Model answer:** The tax raises the cost of labour to employers, shifting the labour demand curve down by the amount of the tax. In the short run, employers pay the tax and wages may not adjust. Over time, reduced labour demand puts downward pressure on wages. Workers accept lower pre-tax wages because the alternative (unemployment) is worse. The new equilibrium has lower wages for workers and higher costs for employers, with the split determined by relative elasticities. When labour supply is relatively inelastic (workers can't easily leave the market), workers bear most of the burden because employers can more easily pass the cost through via lower wages. The legal incidence (who writes the cheque) is irrelevant — economic incidence depends on elasticities.

---

**4. Think About It**
> What would happen to wages and employment if a monopsonist suddenly faced competition from a new firm entering the same labour market?

**Model answer:** Under monopsony, the firm restricts employment below the competitive level (hires where MRP = MLC > w) and pays wages below the competitive wage. A new entrant forces competition for workers. As the two firms bid for labour, wages rise toward the competitive level. Employment also increases because the monopsony restriction is eroded. In the limit (many competing firms), wages equal MRP and employment reaches the competitive level. This is why monopsony power depends on labour market concentration — more employers mean more competition for workers, higher wages, and more employment. It also explains why some mergers that reduce the number of employers in a market can harm workers even without explicit collusion.

---

**5. Discussion**
> Why might monopsony power be more common than we think? Consider industries where workers have few alternative employers, even if the employer is not literally the only firm in town.

**Model answer:** Modern monopsony doesn't require a literal company town. Sources of monopsony power include: (1) Geographic concentration — rural areas or small cities may have few employers in a given occupation. (2) Specialization — workers with specialized skills (nurses, teachers, engineers) have fewer relevant employers. (3) Search frictions — job search is costly and time-consuming, giving current employers wage-setting power. (4) Non-compete agreements — legally restrict mobility. (5) Employer collusion — tacit or explicit agreements not to poach workers (e.g., Silicon Valley wage-fixing scandal). (6) Benefits lock-in — employer-provided pensions or health insurance raise switching costs. Recent empirical evidence using HHI measures of local labour market concentration finds significant monopsony effects in many markets, with wages 5-15% lower in highly concentrated markets.

---

**6. Discussion**
> If minimum wages do not effectively reduce poverty, what alternative policies might be more effective at helping low-income workers, and why?

**Model answer:** Minimum wages are poorly targeted at poverty because many minimum wage workers are secondary earners in non-poor households (e.g., teenagers). More effective alternatives include: (1) Earned income tax credits (like the WITB/CWB) — targeted at low-income families, increase work incentives, and don't raise employer costs. (2) Wage subsidies — lower the cost of hiring low-wage workers while raising their take-home pay. (3) In-kind transfers — child care subsidies, housing assistance targeted at low-income families. (4) Education and training programs — raise productivity and wages permanently rather than mandating a price floor. The key insight is that poverty is a household-level concept while minimum wages are an individual-level instrument — better tools target the household directly. A combination of minimum wages with earned income supplements is often more effective than either alone.

## Human Capital (hcapital)

**1. Discussion**
> The data show that university degree holders have higher participation rates and lower unemployment rates than those with less education. Why might we observe these patterns even during economic downturns — is it purely a productivity story, or could other factors be at play?

**Model answer:** Multiple factors beyond productivity: (1) Human capital theory — more education increases productivity, making these workers more valuable and less likely to be laid off. (2) Signalling — degrees signal ability, motivation, and conscientiousness to employers, making educated workers preferred even when the degree didn't directly increase productivity. (3) Occupation sorting — educated workers are more likely to be in white-collar, salaried positions that are less cyclically sensitive than hourly production jobs. (4) Quasi-fixed costs — employers invest more in training educated workers, making layoffs more costly. (5) Selection — inherently more motivated/able individuals both obtain degrees and have better labour market outcomes. (6) Networks — university provides professional networks that help with job search. During downturns, the education premium often *increases* because less-educated workers face disproportionate layoffs.

---

**2. Think About It**
> Suppose the government introduces a large tuition subsidy that covers 80% of university costs, but simultaneously raises the top marginal income tax rate. What is the net effect on optimal schooling in the human capital model?

**Model answer:** Two opposing effects on the marginal benefit/cost calculus: (1) The tuition subsidy dramatically reduces direct costs of schooling, shifting the marginal cost curve down — this encourages more schooling. (2) The higher tax rate reduces the after-tax return to education (the earnings premium is taxed more heavily), shifting the marginal benefit curve down — this discourages schooling. The net effect depends on magnitudes. If the subsidy is large and the tax increase is modest, schooling increases. But if the tax rate is very high, it can fully offset the subsidy. The key insight is that both costs and returns matter — subsidizing education while heavily taxing its returns can be self-defeating. In practice, the tuition subsidy likely dominates because it affects all students while the top tax rate only affects high earners (and only in the future).

---

**3. Think About It**
> In the signalling model, what would happen if a new technology made it costless for employers to observe worker productivity before hiring? Would anyone still invest in education under the signalling framework?

**Model answer:** If employers can perfectly observe productivity, the signalling value of education drops to zero — there is nothing to signal because the information is already known. Under pure signalling, no one would invest in education (since education doesn't increase productivity in this model, only signals it). However, in practice: (1) Education likely does both — it signals ability AND builds human capital. (2) Even with perfect observation of current productivity, education might signal future learning capacity. (3) Education has consumption value (people enjoy learning). The thought experiment reveals the key empirical question: how much of the return to education is human capital (would survive perfect information) versus signalling (would disappear)? Estimates from sheepskin effects and credential experiments suggest signalling accounts for 20-30% of the return.

---

**4. Discussion**
> Twin studies and IV studies both estimate returns to schooling of about 7-10%, which is lower than the OLS estimate. Does this mean ability bias is the whole story, or could there be biases working in the opposite direction that partially offset it?

**Model answer:** Ability bias is not the whole story. Downward biases also exist: (1) Measurement error in schooling — self-reported years of education contain noise, which biases OLS toward zero (attenuation bias). IV and twin methods can exacerbate this. (2) Discount rate bias — individuals with high discount rates invest less in schooling AND may have lower earnings for other reasons (less patient, less future-oriented), biasing OLS downward. (3) Comparative advantage — individuals who leave school early may have had lower returns to schooling specifically (not just lower ability generally). The fact that IV estimates (7-10%) are not dramatically lower than OLS estimates (~10-12%) suggests that upward ability bias and downward measurement error/discount rate bias roughly offset each other. Card (2001) argues the IV estimates capture the return for marginal students (compliers), who may have higher returns than average.

---

**5. Think About It**
> A tech company pays for an employee's MBA degree. Under the general vs. specific training framework, who should pay for this training, and why? What does it suggest if the firm requires the employee to stay for two years after completing the degree?

**Model answer:** An MBA is general training — the skills are valuable to many employers. Under Becker's framework, workers should pay for general training (through lower wages during training) because competitive firms cannot recoup the investment if the worker leaves. Yet here the firm pays — this suggests: (1) The labour market is not perfectly competitive — the firm has some monopsony power and can pay below MRP after training. (2) The two-year stay requirement is a contractual mechanism to recoup the investment — essentially converting general training into temporarily specific training by restricting mobility. (3) Information asymmetries — the current employer knows more about the worker's ability than outside firms (Acemoglu and Pischke), allowing it to pay below the worker's true post-training MRP. This explains why firms invest in general training more than Becker's model predicts.

## Discrimination (discrim)

**1. Discussion**
> The gender earnings gap has narrowed over time but has not closed. Why might we observe a persistent gap even as women's educational attainment has surpassed men's in many countries?

**Model answer:** Education is necessary but not sufficient to close the gap: (1) Field of study matters — women are still underrepresented in high-paying STEM and business fields. (2) Experience and tenure — career interruptions for childbearing/rearing reduce women's accumulated experience. (3) Hours worked — many high-paying occupations reward long hours disproportionately (Goldin's "greedy jobs" hypothesis). (4) Occupational segregation — women remain concentrated in lower-paying occupations even with more education. (5) Discrimination — both taste-based and statistical discrimination persist. (6) Negotiation and promotion — research shows gender differences in negotiation outcomes and promotion rates. (7) Motherhood penalty — mothers face both real productivity effects (time out of labour force) and discriminatory effects (perceived as less committed). The gap is multidimensional and no single factor explains it.

---

**2. Think About It**
> In Becker's model, discriminating firms sacrifice profits by not hiring equally productive women at lower wages. What would happen to taste-based discrimination in the long run if product markets are perfectly competitive?

**Model answer:** In the long run with perfect competition, taste-based discrimination should be competed away. Non-discriminating firms hire women at lower wages, achieving lower costs and higher profits. They expand and gain market share. Discriminating firms face higher labour costs, earn lower profits, and eventually exit the market (or are acquired). In equilibrium, only non-discriminating firms survive, and the wage gap disappears. This is the "market competition erodes discrimination" hypothesis. However, this prediction clearly doesn't match reality, suggesting: (1) Product markets are not perfectly competitive (market power insulates discriminating firms). (2) Customer or co-worker discrimination persists. (3) Adjustment is very slow. (4) Statistical discrimination, which is profit-consistent, may be a larger factor.

---

**3. Think About It**
> Under statistical discrimination, a high-ability woman and a high-ability man both score well on an employment test, but the signal is noisier for women. What would happen to the wage gap between these two workers if the employer could conduct a longer, more thorough evaluation of all candidates?

**Model answer:** The wage gap would narrow or disappear. Statistical discrimination arises because employers use group averages or perceived group-level signal reliability to make inferences about individuals when individual information is imperfect. A more thorough evaluation reduces noise in the signal for all candidates, making group membership less informative relative to individual performance. As the employer learns more about each individual's true productivity, they rely less on group-level statistics. In the limit (perfect individual information), statistical discrimination disappears entirely — wages equal individual productivity regardless of group membership. This implies that policies improving information (longer probation periods, blind auditions, structured interviews) can reduce statistical discrimination without regulating wages or hiring directly.

---

**4. Discussion**
> In a Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition, the unexplained component is often interpreted as discrimination. Why might this interpretation overstate or understate the true amount of discrimination?

**Model answer:** Overstate: (1) Omitted variables — any productivity-related variable not included in the regression (e.g., motivation, career ambition, specific skills) gets absorbed into the unexplained component. (2) Measurement error in included variables — poorly measured experience or education inflates the unexplained portion. (3) Legitimate compensating differentials — if women choose jobs with better non-wage attributes (flexibility, safety), lower wages reflect preferences, not discrimination. Understate: (1) Pre-market discrimination — if discrimination affects women's education, experience, or occupational choices, these "explained" differences are themselves the result of discrimination. (2) Feedback effects — if women expect discrimination, they invest less in human capital, which shows up as an "explained" difference. (3) Occupational segregation — controlling for occupation may "explain away" discrimination that operates through occupational steering. The decomposition is descriptive, not causal — it tells us where the gap is, not why.

---

**5. Discussion**
> If you were designing a single policy to reduce the gender pay gap, would you target demand-side discrimination (e.g., employer behaviour) or supply-side factors (e.g., occupational choice, human capital accumulation)? Why?

**Model answer:** Strong arguments exist for both sides. Demand-side: Pay transparency laws, pay equity legislation, and anti-discrimination enforcement directly address employer behaviour. Audit studies consistently show hiring discrimination exists. If the unexplained gap is large, demand-side interventions have high potential impact. Supply-side: Subsidized child care, parental leave reform, and STEM pipeline programs address root causes of occupational segregation and career interruptions. If most of the gap is "explained" by experience, occupation, and hours, supply-side interventions address a larger share. Best answer: The most effective single policy may be one that bridges both — universal affordable child care, which (1) reduces career interruptions (supply-side), (2) weakens the statistical basis for employers discriminating against women of childbearing age (demand-side), and (3) has been shown empirically to narrow the gap (Quebec evidence). In practice, a comprehensive approach targeting both sides is most effective.
